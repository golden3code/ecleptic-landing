How many hours of sleep do you really need?

7 to 9 hours for most adults, but the real answer depends on you. What the data actually says, and how to find your own sleep need in just two weeks.

By Auguste Phily-Priou, founder of Ecleptic  ·  updated September 30, 2026

The short answer: between 7 and 9 hours for nearly all adults. That's the range set by the National Sleep Foundation; the American Academy of Sleep Medicine, for its part, recommends 7 hours or more, after a review of 5,314 scientific articles. Regularly getting less than 7 hours raises measurable risks — metabolism, immunity, mood, accidents. More than 9 hours in a healthy adult is rarely a need, except in young adults: more often it's a sign of sleep debt being paid back, an illness, or poor-quality sleep being made up for in quantity.

The honest answer: it depends on you, and the variation is bigger than people think. Your actual need depends largely on your genetics. Some people function perfectly on 7 hours. Others need 9 hours to perform at the same level. True natural “short sleepers” — less than 6 hours with no deficit at all — do exist, but they're extremely rare. Statistically, you're not one of them.

Do you really need 8 hours of sleep?

The 8-hour figure isn't wrong; it's misunderstood. It's a population average, not an individual prescription. Saying “you need 8 hours of sleep” is like saying everyone should wear the average shoe size: true on average, and wrong for half of people.

What research shows robustly is the shape of the curve: health risks follow a U. A 2010 meta-analysis (16 studies, 1.38 million participants) links short nights to a 12% higher risk of death, and long nights — most often 9 hours or more — to a 30% higher risk. The low point sits around 7 to 8 hours, and very long nights are probably more often a symptom than a cause. Your personal target lies somewhere in that dip, and it shifts with the phases of your life: training load, stress, illness, sleep debt to catch up on.

How much sleep do you need if you work out?

Exercise increases your sleep need, because most of the repair happens overnight: growth hormone release during deep sleep, protein synthesis, consolidation of motor learning.

“Sleep extension” studies in athletes are telling: when 11 Stanford basketball players aimed for 10 hours in bed per night for 5 to 7 weeks, they slept nearly 2 hours more, their sprints got faster, their shooting accuracy improved by about 9 percentage points, and their reaction time dropped. Conversely, a single short night reduces maximal strength only modestly — but clearly reduces endurance, motivation and exercise tolerance the very next day.

  • You train 3 to 4 times a week: aim for 7.5 to 8.5 hours of actual sleep.
  • You train 5 times a week or more, or you're preparing for a competition: aim for 8 to 9 hours, naps included.
  • Cutting phase or calorie deficit: lack of sleep burns muscle first — in a 2010 trial (10 adults, 14 days of dieting), going from 8.5 to 5.5 hours in bed increased lean mass loss by 60% and cut the share of fat lost by 55%.

Time in bed vs. time asleep

A detail that changes everything: these numbers refer to actual sleep, not time spent in bed. With a normal sleep efficiency of 85 to 92%, you need about 8 hr 30 min in bed to sleep 7 hr 45 min. If you go to bed at midnight and get up at 7 a.m. thinking you “sleep 7 hours,” you're probably sleeping 6 hr 15 min. It's the most common miscalculation — and it's enough to create an invisible chronic sleep debt.

Signs you're not getting enough sleep

Your body answers the question better than any general rule. The reliable signals:

  • You need an alarm to wake up every day, and it drags you out of deep sleep.
  • You sleep 1.5 hours or more extra on weekends: that's debt being repaid, not laziness.
  • You fall asleep in less than 5 minutes at night — falling asleep instantly isn't a talent, it's a symptom of sleep deprivation.
  • A marked slump in the early afternoon, coffee you can't get through the morning without, irritability for no clear reason.

None of these signs alone is proof. All four together are.

How to find out how much sleep you need: the two-week test

The most reliable method requires no equipment, just a calm stretch of time (a vacation, or two weeks without big deadlines):

  • Go to bed at the same time every night, chosen to leave you at least 8.5 hours before you'd need to be up.
  • No alarm. Let your body wake up on its own.
  • Ignore the first few nights: you're paying back your debt first, so the durations will be inflated. A 2016 Japanese study showed that a single hour of sleep debt takes about four days to recover from.
  • After 10 to 14 days, the time you sleep before waking up naturally stabilizes. That number is your real need.

After that, the job is to protect that amount on weekdays — and that's where regular sleep times matter almost as much as duration itself: going to bed and getting up at fixed times stabilizes your circadian clock and improves sleep quality for the same number of hours.

Key takeaways

  • 7 to 9 hours of actual sleep for almost everyone; the top of the range if you train seriously.
  • “8 hours” is an average, not your prescription: your need is personal, and you can measure it.
  • Plan on 30 to 60 minutes more in bed than your sleep target.
  • Waking up naturally, steady energy during the day, no massive catch-up on weekends: that's what enough sleep looks like.

Frequently asked questions

How many hours of sleep does an adult need per night?

Between 7 and 9 hours of actual sleep according to the National Sleep Foundation, and at least 7 hours according to the American Academy of Sleep Medicine. If you train seriously, aim for the top of the range: 8 to 9 hours.

Is 6 hours of sleep enough?

Almost never. True short sleepers — less than 6 hours with no deficit at all — are extremely rare. Below 7 hours on a regular basis, measurable risks go up: metabolism, immunity, mood, accidents.

How do you find out how much sleep you really need?

Two weeks with a fixed bedtime and no alarm. Ignore the first few nights (you're paying back your debt: one hour of debt takes about four days to recover from), then the time you sleep before waking up naturally stabilizes: that number is your real need.
