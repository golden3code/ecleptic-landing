Resonance breathing: a breathing exercise that calms stress

Breathing at six breaths per minute for five minutes calms your nervous system and raises your HRV. The 5-5 pattern, why it works and when to use it.

By Auguste Phily-Priou, founder of Ecleptic

The short answer: breathe slowly, at about 6 breaths per minute, for roughly 5 minutes. That's resonance breathing, also called coherent breathing or cardiac coherence. At this precise pace, your breathing syncs up with the natural oscillations of your heart rate, which engages the parasympathetic brake — the “calm” branch of your nervous system — and brings tension down within minutes. It's one of the rare anti-stress tools that is free, immediate, equipment-free and validated all at once.

The classic pattern fits in one sentence: breathe in for 5 seconds, breathe out for 5 seconds, without forcing, letting your belly expand. Ten seconds per cycle, six cycles per minute. That's it.

What happens in your body during resonance breathing?

Your heart rate isn't fixed: it speeds up slightly when you breathe in and slows down when you breathe out. By setting your breathing to around six cycles per minute, you deliberately amplify this swing and bring it into phase — that's the “resonance.” Researchers Paul Lehrer and Richard Gevirtz (2014) describe it: at around 0.1 hertz, or six breaths per minute, heart and breathing oscillate exactly in phase, and heart rate variations reach their maximum amplitude. Two concrete effects:

  • Your vagus nerve takes over. Slow exhalation stimulates the parasympathetic system: your heart slows with each breath out, and your body leaves alert mode.
  • Your heart rate variability goes up. HRV — the marker of your nervous system's state — rises clearly. A 2022 meta-analysis (223 studies) confirms it: vagally mediated HRV increases during slow breathing, right after a session, and at the end of a program of several sessions. It's the visible sign that the brake is working.

This isn't vague relaxation: it's a mechanical lever on your physiology, and it works in real time.

How to do resonance breathing (it's really simple)

  • Get settled sitting down, back straight, shoulders relaxed. No special place needed.
  • Breathe in for 5 seconds through your nose, letting your belly expand, then breathe out for 5 seconds, gently. The exhale is the part that calms you — don't rush it.
  • Keep it up for 5 minutes. An app, a video or a simple visual guide that rises and falls helps you hold the pace without counting.
  • If 5 seconds feels uncomfortable, start a little shorter (with the inhale slightly shorter than the exhale): what matters is the slow pace and the longer exhale, not to-the-second precision.

Two mistakes often spoil the exercise: breathing too hard — the goal is a soft, steady breath, definitely not hyperventilation — and getting hung up on the numbers. If counting stresses you out, let a visual or an app guide you and forget the timer: your body finds the rhythm on its own within a few cycles.

The “3-6-5” rule of thumb

In France, the method is often summed up by three easy-to-remember numbers: 3 times a day, 6 breaths per minute, for 5 minutes. Three daily sessions — typically when you wake up, before lunch and in the late afternoon — are enough to build the habit and smooth out stress across the whole day. It's a practical benchmark, not a protocol that has been tested as such: twice is better than once, and once is better than zero. But this format gives you a concrete framework when “breathe to calm down” stays too vague to stick with.

When should you use resonance breathing?

  • In an emergency. Before speaking in public, in a traffic jam, after bad news: even a single 5-minute session brings the pressure down. It's your pocket “reset” button.
  • As maintenance. Practiced regularly — ideally a few minutes a day, for example morning and evening — it trains your nervous system to return to calm faster, and becomes part of real stress and recovery management. A 2017 meta-analysis (24 studies, 484 participants) links this training, guided by a biofeedback device, to a clear drop in perceived stress and anxiety.

One especially worthwhile moment: the evening, a few minutes before bed, to switch your body into rest mode. And in the bigger picture, it's one of the documented levers for keeping chronic stress from settling in.

Does resonance breathing really work? What it isn't

Let's be honest about the promises. Slow breathing calms acute stress and improves how you feel — that's real and measurable, but modest in size: a 2023 meta-analysis (12 randomized trials, 785 adults) found a small-to-medium effect of breathing techniques on stress, and its authors warn about the gap between the hype and the evidence. What it isn't is a miracle cure that “heals” anxiety, melts fat or replaces sleep, as some sellers of methods sometimes claim. It's a regulation tool, excellent at its job, to combine with the fundamentals: moving, sleeping, eating well. Used for what it is, it does you a huge service at zero cost.

Key takeaways

  • Six breaths per minute (5 s in, 5 s out) for 5 minutes engage the parasympathetic system and raise your HRV.
  • A single session already calms you; regular practice trains your nervous system to return to calm over the long term.
  • Free, immediate, no equipment: pull it out in an emergency and build it into your evening.

Frequently asked questions

What is resonance breathing (cardiac coherence)?

A slow breathing technique at about 6 cycles per minute (5 seconds in, 5 seconds out) for 5 minutes. It syncs your breathing with your heart rhythm, engages the parasympathetic system and calms stress quickly.

How do you do resonance breathing?

Sitting, back straight: breathe in for 5 seconds through your nose, letting your belly expand, breathe out gently for 5 seconds, and keep it up for 5 minutes. A visual guide that rises and falls helps you hold the pace without counting.

Does resonance breathing really reduce stress?

Yes, for acute stress and how you feel: slow exhalation stimulates the vagus nerve and raises heart rate variability. It's not a miracle cure, but a free, immediate regulation tool.
