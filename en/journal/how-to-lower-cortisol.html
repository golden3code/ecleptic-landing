Cortisol: how to lower it and ease chronic stress

Cortisol isn't the enemy: the real problem is chronic elevation. Sleep, the right dose of exercise, slow breathing — the levers that work, and the scams.

By Auguste Phily-Priou, founder of Ecleptic

The short answer: first, stop demonizing cortisol. It's a perfectly normal and vital hormone — it wakes you up in the morning, mobilizes your energy, manages inflammation. It follows a circadian rhythm: high when you wake up (it's what gets you out of bed), it declines through the day and hits its lowest point in the evening. The problem has never been cortisol itself. It's chronic elevation — when stress never comes back down and your body stays on alert for weeks on end.

In other words, you're not trying to “eliminate” your cortisol (that would be dangerous), but to restore its rhythm: a clear peak in the morning, a real decline in the evening. That rhythm is what counts: a 2017 meta-analysis (80 studies) links a flatter cortisol curve between morning and evening to poorer health, physical as well as mental. And the levers to restore it are well known, boring and effective — nothing to do with the “cortisol detox” supplements sold online.

How to lower cortisol: the real levers, by impact

  • Sleep, far ahead of everything else. Lack of sleep raises cortisol as early as the next day: in a study published in Sleep in 1997, a shortened night raised the following evening's cortisol by 37% (45% after a sleepless night). And a lasting sleep debt keeps it high: after six nights of 4 hours, evening cortisol was higher than after recovery nights (The Lancet, 1999). Getting enough sleep, on a regular schedule, is by far the number one lever.
  • Physical activity — in the right dose. Regular exercise improves stress regulation over the long term. But watch the dose effect: too much training without recovery becomes one more stressor and throws hormonal systems off — and cortisol alone isn't enough to spot it: the 2013 European and American consensus statement on overtraining notes that no marker, hormonal or otherwise, is universally accepted. Overtraining is a chronic stressor in its own right.
  • Slow breathing. A few minutes of resonance breathing engage the parasympathetic brake and help your body leave alert mode — one of the rare “come down” buttons you can press at will.
  • Reasonable caffeine. Caffeine raises cortisol, and habit blunts that response without erasing it: in a 2005 trial (96 participants), morning coffee no longer triggered a cortisol response in regular drinkers, but afternoon coffee set it off again. One coffee in the morning isn't a problem; six coffees through 5 p.m. keep you on alert and sabotage your night on top of it.
  • Managing everyday stress. What really weighs on you is stress that never switches off. Anything that creates real breaks — nature, social connection, screen-free time, recovery worthy of the name — counts for more than any supplement.

Light and rhythm: the detail that helps

Since cortisol is a rhythm hormone, you can help it along by strengthening your circadian cues: bright light in the morning (ideally outdoors) sets the peak at the right time, and a dim evening without harsh screens lets cortisol come down and melatonin rise. Getting up and going to bed at consistent times does the rest. You're not “fighting” your cortisol: you're giving it a clean clock again.

Is “cortisol belly” real?

Social media has invented “cortisol bellies” and “cortisol faces” that a three-week cure would supposedly melt away. The reality is more down to earth: in a healthy person, weight gain comes down first to energy balance and sleep, not to cortisol that an herbal tea could bring down. Truly pathological cortisol excess does exist — Cushing's syndrome — but it's a rare disease (about 3 new cases per million people per year, according to a 2019 Swedish study), diagnosed by a doctor, not a condition you assign yourself after watching a video. If you're doing well overall, focus on the fundamentals rather than on your “levels.”

Beware of cortisol scams

“Cortisol” has become a magic word in wellness marketing. Be wary of:

  • “Anti-cortisol” or “detox” supplements with spectacular promises: serious evidence is lacking, and artificially lowering a vital hormone isn't a healthy goal anyway.
  • Saliva cortisol tests sold outside any medical setting, whose interpretation out of context tells you nothing useful.
  • The idea that “high cortisol” on its own explains your fatigue or your extra pounds: the reality is almost always multifactorial, and first and foremost a matter of sleep and load.

A word of caution: if you have signs that go beyond ordinary stress — major fatigue, unexplained weight changes, high blood pressure, marked disturbances — what you need isn't an article or a supplement, it's a doctor. Some hormonal disorders do exist and are diagnosed medically.

Key takeaways

  • Cortisol is normal and useful; the problem is chronic elevation, not the hormone itself.
  • The real levers: enough sleep on a regular schedule, the right dose of exercise, slow breathing, moderate caffeine, real breaks.
  • Steer clear of “cortisol detox” supplements; if you notice unusual signs, see a doctor.

Frequently asked questions

How can you lower cortisol naturally?

By restoring its rhythm, not suppressing it: sleep enough and at regular times, exercise in the right dose, breathe slowly, moderate your caffeine and create real breaks from stress. Sleep is the number one lever, by far.

Do anti-cortisol supplements work?

Serious evidence is lacking, and artificially lowering a vital hormone isn't a healthy goal. Be wary of “cortisol detox” marketing: the real levers are sleep, training load and stress management.

Is high cortisol dangerous?

Cortisol is normally high in the morning and low in the evening; it's chronic elevation that's the problem. If you notice unusual signs — major fatigue, weight changes, high blood pressure — see a doctor rather than reaching for a supplement.
