Full body vs split: which workout should you choose?

Full body or split? The right choice depends on how often you train: 3 sessions, go full body; 4 or more, pick a split. And the rule that beats all the rest.

By Auguste Phily-Priou, founder of Ecleptic

The short answer: it depends on how many days a week you can train. If you're a beginner or aiming for 3 sessions a week, full body (your whole body every session) is the best choice. If you go up to 4 days or more, a split — upper/lower or push/pull/legs — makes more sense. There's no magic program: the right one is the program that follows one simple rule and that you can stick with over time.

Here's that rule: train each muscle about 2 times a week. A 2016 meta-analysis found two sessions per muscle more effective than one; its 2019 update (25 studies) added nuance: when total volume is equal, frequency changes little. The real benefit of hitting a muscle twice is fitting in enough volume without endless workouts. Everything else — the program's name, the order of exercises — is just logistics to get you there.

Full body: the best choice for 3 sessions or fewer

In a full body program, every session works your whole body. With 3 sessions a week, each muscle is trained 3 times — comfortably within the right range. It's the simplest, most efficient approach for beginners, and for anyone who can't make it to the gym more than three times a week.

  • Good frequency guaranteed, even with few sessions.
  • Resilient to the unexpected: if you skip a session, every muscle has still been trained that week.
  • Less crippling soreness per session, because the volume for each muscle is spread out.

The only downside: beyond 3 days, full body becomes hard to recover from, because you keep hitting the same muscles without giving them time to recover. That's where a split takes over.

Split training: when you train more often

“Split” simply means you divide your body across several sessions. Two splits have proven themselves:

  • Upper/lower: one upper-body session, one lower-body session, alternating. Over 4 sessions, each half gets trained twice. Simple and effective.
  • Push/pull/legs: push (chest, shoulders, triceps), pull (back, biceps), legs. Over 6 sessions, each group gets trained twice. Ideal when you have a lot of volume to fit in.

Then there's the old “one muscle a day” split, often called the bro split (chest on Monday, back on Tuesday, and so on), which trains each muscle only once a week. It's not a disaster: when volume is equal, meta-analyses don't see a big difference. Its flaw is practical: all of a muscle's volume is packed into one session, which quickly drags on forever, and one skipped session wipes that muscle off your week. For most people, it's better to aim for a frequency of 2 times a week.

What does a full body or split week look like?

To make it concrete, here are three simple layouts:

  • 3 days (full body): Monday, Wednesday, Friday — each session combines a leg movement, a push and a pull.
  • 4 days (upper/lower): upper body on Monday, lower on Tuesday, upper on Thursday, lower on Friday.
  • 6 days (push/pull/legs): two push / pull / legs cycles spread across the week.

None of these plans is “better” in absolute terms: the right one is the one that fits your real schedule and that you keep repeating instead of giving up after a month.

What matters more than your workout split

The full body vs split choice makes much less difference than people think: a 2024 meta-analysis (14 studies, 392 participants) found no difference in strength or muscle growth between the two when volume was equal. Three factors weigh far more than the program's label:

  • Adherence. The best program is the one you follow week after week. A perfect plan you abandon after three weeks is worth nothing.
  • Progressive overload. Adding weight, reps or sets over time is the real driver of progress — it's the non-negotiable principle, whatever the program, and the first recommendation in the American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 position stand.
  • Recovery. Sleep, protein, managing your training load. Without it, no program works.

So, full body or split: which should you choose?

Decide based on your reality, not a theoretical ideal: 2-3 days a week → full body; 4 days → upper/lower; 5-6 days → push/pull/legs. As for frequency itself, the American College of Sports Medicine recommends 2 to 3 sessions a week for beginners, 3 to 4 for intermediate lifters and 4 to 5 for advanced lifters. Choose the split that keeps you showing up consistently and lets you keep adding load. The number of reps per set is something you adjust within the program, whichever one you choose.

Key takeaways

  • Beginner or 3 sessions → full body; 4 days → upper/lower; 5-6 days → push/pull/legs.
  • The rule that comes first: train each muscle about 2 times a week, mainly to fit in enough volume.
  • Adherence, progressive overload and recovery matter more than the program's name.

Frequently asked questions

Full body or split: which is better?

It depends on how often you train: full body if you train 3 times a week or less, a split (upper/lower or push/pull/legs) from 4 days a week. The common rule: train each muscle about 2 times a week.

Is a full body workout good for beginners?

Yes, it's actually the best choice. With 3 sessions a week, each muscle is trained 3 times, which is plenty — and the program holds up if you skip a session.

Should you train one muscle group per day?

It's neither necessary nor the most practical option. When volume is equal, studies show no big difference, but packing all of a muscle's work into one session quickly becomes a slog, and one missed session wipes that muscle off your week. You're better off with a split that trains each group twice.
