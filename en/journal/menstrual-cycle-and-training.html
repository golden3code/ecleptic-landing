Menstrual cycle and exercise: should you adjust your training?

Menstrual cycle and training: the science is limited and highly individual. Listen to your body, not a calendar. And losing your period is never trivial.

By Auguste Phily-Priou, founder of Ecleptic

The short answer: maybe, but not according to a rigid, universal rule. The science on this is still limited and, above all, extremely individual: the variability from one person to the next outweighs anything you might want to generalize. Many people report more energy in the first half of the cycle and more heaviness before their period — but that remains an average tendency, not a law that would apply to you specifically.

So the right approach isn't to follow a theoretical calendar you found online, but to listen to your body and your data, and adjust intensity based on how you actually feel. Your cycle is one factor among others — just like your sleep or your stress — not a training program in itself.

What the science says about training and your cycle (and what it doesn't)

Let's be honest about the state of knowledge:

  • Solid studies are lacking: the topic was long neglected by research, and study protocols remain inconsistent.
  • The average effects measured are small compared with the differences between individuals. A 2020 meta-analysis (78 studies) found only a "trivial" drop in performance at the very start of the cycle, during menstruation, based on low-quality evidence — and recommends a personalized approach rather than general rules. In other words, your cycle phase explains far less of how you feel on a given day than your sleep, your nutrition or your recent training load.
  • No solid data justifies systematically holding back your training at a particular time of the month. For strength training, a 2023 review considers it premature to claim that hormonal fluctuations affect strength or muscle gain. You can train and make progress throughout your entire cycle.

Bottom line: be wary of methods that promise "cycle-synced training" built around the 4 phases with clockwork precision. It sells well, but it greatly overstates what the science can claim today.

Which phase of your cycle gives you the most energy? Common patterns

That said, many women describe a fairly consistent experience: in a 2021 survey of 6,812 exercising women, 86% reported cycle-related fatigue and 91% reported mood changes or anxiety. It's an experience worth observing in your own case:

  • Follicular phase (from the first day of your period to ovulation, roughly the first half): once your period is over, often more energy, and good tolerance for intense sessions and heavy loads.
  • Luteal and premenstrual phase (second half, before your period): sometimes more fatigue, recovery that drags, lower motivation, poorer sleep.
  • During your period: highly variable — some women are bothered by it, others feel perfectly fine and perform normally.

The takeaway: treat this as a hypothesis to test on yourself, not as an imposed truth. Note how you feel over two or three cycles and see whether a pattern really emerges for you.

The method that works: how you feel and your data

Rather than following a rigid calendar, steer by feel and by objective signals:

  • On days when the energy is there, make the most of it for demanding sessions and heavy loads.
  • On heavy days, scale back without guilt: lower the volume or intensity, but keep moving. A moderate session is always better than a forced, failed one.
  • Lean on your morning vitals. A readiness score that combines resting heart rate, heart rate variability and sleep captures your actual fatigue that day, whatever its cause — your cycle included — without you having to guess.

It's more reliable than a theoretical model, because it measures your actual state instead of assuming it.

Losing your period from exercise: a signal never to dismiss

An important point, one that goes well beyond training comfort. In an athlete, the loss of periods (amenorrhea) is not a sign of good fitness, nor a "normal" consequence of heavy training. Most often it signals an energy deficit — not enough fuel to cover what you burn — a mechanism at the heart of what's called Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (REDs), the subject of an International Olympic Committee consensus statement updated in 2023. According to the American College of Sports Medicine, it's this low energy availability, often unintentional, that disrupts the cycle and weakens bones. Over time, it affects hormones, bone density and overall health. And it isn't limited to elite athletes: it also threatens dedicated recreational athletes who, without meaning to, eat too little for everything they burn.

If your periods stop or become very irregular while you're training, it's not a minor detail: start by checking that you're eating enough for what you burn, and above all talk to a doctor about it. It's a reason to book an appointment, not something to let slide — and a common cause of persistent fatigue in female athletes.

Key takeaways

  • The science is limited and, above all, highly individual: there's no universal rule for syncing your training to your cycle.
  • Listen to your body and your data, and adjust intensity based on how you feel rather than a theoretical calendar.
  • Losing your period as an athlete is never "normal": it's often a sign of an energy deficit, and a reason to see a doctor.

Frequently asked questions

Should you adjust your training to your menstrual cycle?

There's no universal rule: the science is limited and highly individual. Rather than a theoretical calendar, adjust intensity based on how you feel and your data for the day. You can train and make progress throughout your whole cycle.

When do you have the most energy in your menstrual cycle?

Many people report more energy once their period is over, up to ovulation, and more fatigue before their period. But the variability from one person to the next dominates: watch what actually happens for you.

Is losing your period because of exercise serious?

Yes, it's a signal not to dismiss. Losing your period as an athlete often reflects an energy deficit and can affect hormones and bones. It's a reason to see a doctor, not a normal consequence of training.
