Stretching: before or after exercise, and what does it do?

Before: dynamic stretches, because long static holds right before exercise sap your strength. After: for flexibility. And no, it won't really prevent soreness.

By Auguste Phily-Priou, founder of Ecleptic

The short answer: before exercise, stretch on the move (dynamic stretches, as part of your warm-up); save long static stretches for afterward or for separate sessions, when your goal is to gain flexibility. And above all: stretching prevents neither soreness nor most injuries. That doesn't mean it's useless — it means it was long credited with powers it doesn't have.

Should you stretch before a workout? Dynamic, not static

A good warm-up prepares your muscles, joints and nervous system for the movement ahead. That means dynamic stretches: walking lunges, leg swings, rotations, high knees, lighter versions of the exercise you're about to do.

By contrast, holding a static stretch for 60 seconds or more per muscle right before a strength or power effort can temporarily reduce your performance — less strength, less explosiveness for a few minutes. A review of 106 studies (2012) puts the threshold at around 60 seconds, and a 2016 review puts the drop at 4.6% on average beyond that, versus 1.1% below; dynamic stretching, on the other hand, slightly improves performance (+1.3%). The effect is modest and short-lived, but when you're trying to lift heavy or sprint, there's no reason to sabotage yourself. So save long static holds for another time.

  • Before lifting or sprinting: 5 to 10 minutes of dynamic work, gradually building up the intensity.
  • What you want is to be warm and mobile, not loose and floppy.

Stretching after exercise (or separately): for flexibility

Static stretches have a real use: gaining range of motion over the long term. Done regularly, they increase flexibility: the American College of Sports Medicine recommends stretching each major muscle group at least 2 days a week, for a total of about 60 seconds per exercise. After a session, they also feel good and help you relax — they don't speed up muscle repair, but they're pleasant and they do no harm.

The real lever isn't stretching after exercise out of habit, it's training mobility as a separate workout when you need it for your lifts and movements: squat depth, shoulders, hips. There, the range of motion you gain is useful and lasting.

Does stretching prevent soreness or injuries? The myth to bust

This is the most important point, because it's the #1 reason people stretch — and it's wrong.

  • Stretching doesn't prevent muscle soreness. It's one of the most consistently replicated findings in the research: according to the 2011 Cochrane review (12 studies, including one with 2,377 participants), stretching before exercise reduces next-day soreness by half a point on a 100-point scale, and stretching afterward by one point. Nothing you'd notice. What actually helps is light movement, sleep and protein.
  • It doesn't prevent most injuries. What protects you is a good warm-up, a reasonable progression in load, and strength. A 2014 meta-analysis (25 trials, 26,610 participants) found no protective effect from stretching, whereas strength training cut injury risk to less than a third.

So what should you actually do?

  • Before: a dynamic warm-up, always. A few minutes is enough.
  • After: a few gentle stretches if they feel good, without expecting miracles for soreness.
  • Flexibility: if you lack range of motion, make it a separate, regular training goal — like any other progress, it comes with repetition.
  • Fuel matters more than you think: a well-prepared session starts on your plate — what you eat before exercise matters more for your performance than three minutes of stretching.

And remember that flexibility is only one building block among others. What makes a body progress over time is a coherent whole: the right number of workouts per week, sleep, and consistency.

Key takeaways

  • Before exercise: dynamic stretches; long static holds right before can reduce strength and power.
  • After exercise or separately: static stretching is for gaining flexibility, not for recovering faster.
  • Stretching prevents neither soreness nor most injuries — that's not its job.

Frequently asked questions

Should you stretch before or after exercise?

Before: dynamic stretches as part of your warm-up. After, or in dedicated sessions: static stretches to gain flexibility. Holding a static stretch for 60 seconds or more right before a strength effort can reduce your performance.

Does stretching prevent muscle soreness?

No. It's one of the best-established findings: stretching before or after exercise barely changes soreness. What helps is light movement, sleep and enough protein.

Does stretching prevent injuries?

Not most of them. What really protects you is a good warm-up, a reasonable progression in load, and strength — not static stretching before exercise.
