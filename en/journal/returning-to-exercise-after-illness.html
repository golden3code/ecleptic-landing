Returning to exercise after illness: the rules to follow

The above/below-the-neck rule, the gradual 60-70% restart and the fever warning you must never ignore: how to return to exercise after illness safely.

By Auguste Phily-Priou, founder of Ecleptic

The short answer: use the “above / below the neck” rule. If your symptoms are above the neck — runny nose, slightly scratchy throat, sneezing — light activity is generally fine, and you often even feel better afterward. If you have a fever, all-over body aches, chest symptoms (wet cough, chest tightness) or heavy general fatigue: it's rest, no arguing.

And one safety rule overrides everything else: never do intense exercise with a fever or whole-body symptoms. This isn't excessive caution — it's the point where exercise can become genuinely risky.

Can you exercise with a fever? Why fever changes everything

Intense exercise during an infection with fever exposes you to a rare but serious risk: myocarditis, an inflammation of the heart muscle. A 2000 Swedish review is clear-cut: intense exercise during an infection with fever should always be avoided. A 2021 sports cardiology review also lists training while sick, or returning too early, among the risk factors for myocarditis in elite athletes. That's why the rule is categorical: fever, all-over aches, swollen glands, unusual shortness of breath → you don't train, you recover. At the slightest chest sign — pain, tightness, palpitations, abnormal shortness of breath — or the slightest doubt, see a doctor before returning. That line isn't up for negotiation.

How long should you wait to exercise after being sick?

There's no universal countdown. The 2022 International Olympic Committee (IOC) consensus statement notes that the neck rule rests on expert opinion, and goes by symptoms instead:

For a simple cold, there's no need to stop everything: in a 1998 trial, volunteers with a cold who did 40 minutes of moderate exercise every other day had neither a more severe nor a longer cold. With a fever, wait until it has gone before any return. And after a week-long feverish infection, fully regaining strength and endurance can take several weeks, according to a 1998 review in the International Journal of Sports Medicine.

While you recover, don't neglect the basics that speed up your return: sleep more than usual, stay well hydrated, and eat until you're satisfied — this is definitely not the time to combine a diet with a comeback.

How to return to exercise gradually, without wrecking everything

Once you're better, the temptation is to pick up where you left off. Bad idea: a few days off plus an infection chip away at your capacity, and coming back too hard drags out your recovery. The sensible progression:

  • Start with a test session: 10 to 20 minutes at 60-70% of your usual intensity, the format proposed by the IOC consensus.
  • Build back up in steps, session by session, only if everything goes well during exercise and in the 24 hours after: no chest pain or tightness, no palpitations, no excessive shortness of breath or fatigue, no effort that feels abnormally hard for your usual load. At the slightest of these signs, you stop and see a doctor.
  • Don't try to make up for anything. Missed workouts are missed; trying to “compensate” is the best way to relapse or slide into overtraining.

Your two best objective indicators

How you feel sometimes lies: the urge to get back at it clouds your judgment. Two measurements taken when you wake up are fairly honest:

  • Your resting heart rate. Still several beats above your normal? Your body hasn't finished recovering — give it time. It's also a typical sign of myocarditis in athletes, according to the cardiology review mentioned above.
  • Your heart rate variability. HRV often drops during an infection and climbs back up when you truly recover. Waiting until it returns toward your baseline is a much better guide than the calendar.

One last, very simple test: your first real comeback workout. If moderate effort feels abnormally hard, your heart races for no reason, or you're wiped out the next day, you came back too early. Take a step back, give it another two or three days, and try again. There's never anything to gain from forcing a comeback — and often a relapse at the end of it.

That's exactly the kind of reading a readiness score sums up every morning: when your resting heart rate and HRV are back to your normal, the light turns green. Until then, you're better off waiting.

Key takeaways

  • The above/below-the-neck rule: mild head symptoms → gentle activity; fever, chest, whole body → rest.
  • Never do intense exercise with a fever (risk of myocarditis); at the slightest chest sign or doubt, see a doctor.
  • Come back with a test session at 60-70% of your intensity, then build up in steps, and trust your resting heart rate and HRV rather than your impatience.

Frequently asked questions

When can you return to exercise after being sick?

Use the above/below-the-neck rule: mild head symptoms (nose, throat) → gentle activity is possible; fever, all-over body aches, chest symptoms or heavy fatigue → complete rest.

Can you work out with a fever?

No. Intense exercise with a fever exposes you to a rare but serious risk of myocarditis (inflammation of the heart). At the slightest chest sign or the slightest doubt, see a doctor before returning.

How do you get back into training after an illness?

Start with a 10- to 20-minute test session at 60-70% of your usual intensity, then increase the load session by session if everything goes well during exercise and in the 24 hours after. Also go by your resting heart rate and HRV when you wake up: once they're back to your normal, you can pick up the pace.
