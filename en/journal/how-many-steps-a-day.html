How many steps a day do you really need?

How many steps a day do you really need? Aim for 7,000 to 8,000: that's where most of the benefits are. The 10,000-step goal is a marketing myth.

By Auguste Phily-Priou, founder of Ecleptic

The short answer: aim for 7,000 to 8,000 steps a day. That's where most of the health benefits are concentrated — not at the famous 10,000 steps, a number with nothing scientific about it. Beyond that, the benefit curve gradually flattens: walking more does no harm, but each extra thousand steps pays off less and less.

And if you're far from that, the best news is somewhere else: it's at the bottom of the scale that every step counts most. Going from 3,000 to 5,000 steps does far more, proportionally, than going from 9,000 to 11,000.

Where does the 10,000 steps a day goal come from?

Not from science — from a marketing slogan, as a 2004 review by Tudor-Locke and Bassett already pointed out. In the 1960s, a Japanese company launched a pedometer called the “manpo-kei,” literally “10,000-step meter.” The number was round, catchy and easy to remember. It stuck, and decades later people mistake it for a medical recommendation. It never was one.

Modern data tell a different story. A 2025 meta-analysis (57 studies) links 7,000 steps a day to 47% lower mortality compared with 2,000 steps. Another one, from 2022 (15 cohorts, 47,471 adults), puts the plateau around 6,000-8,000 steps for people 60 and older, and around 8,000-10,000 steps for those under 60. Why does walking matter so much?

Walking doesn't look like much, and that's exactly its strength: it's a background activity you rack up without noticing, with no recovery to plan for, every single day. It's the main contributor to what physiologists call NEAT (non-exercise activity thermogenesis) — the energy you spend on all your movement outside of exercise: walking, climbing stairs, running errands, moving around at work.

According to researcher James Levine, a specialist in the field, NEAT accounts for the vast majority of your daily energy expenditure outside of rest, and it's often what makes the difference between two people on the same training program. Someone who walks a lot burns, every day and with no perceived effort, what a workout would add — except they do it seven days a week.

Are fewer steps still worth it?

The most important message if you're starting from far away: even 4,000 steps are clearly better than sitting still. Among 16,741 women with an average age of 72, about 4,400 steps a day was already associated with 41% lower mortality than 2,700. There's no magic threshold below which “it's pointless.” The relationship is continuous: every extra bit of movement lowers your risk, especially at the bottom of the scale.

  • Under 5,000 steps: you're in the sedentary zone. This is where the smallest amount of walking pays off most — start by adding 1,000 to 2,000 steps.
  • 7,000 to 8,000 steps: the comfortable target where most of the benefits are already locked in.
  • Above 10,000: no harm, but don't force that number on yourself as dogma. Consistency beats one-off performance.

A word on pace: walking briskly — enough to breathe harder without getting out of breath — may add a small bonus, but it remains secondary to your total step count. Among 4,840 American adults, step cadence was no longer linked to mortality once total steps were taken into account. Don't get hung up on speed. To add steps without thinking about it: get off one stop early, take the stairs, take phone calls while walking, or do a ten-minute loop after each meal — that last habit may also help your blood sugar.

Is walking enough exercise?

One caveat: walking is a fantastic base, but it doesn't check every box. It doesn't build muscle strength, and it doesn't push your cardiorespiratory system hard enough to raise your peak endurance much — for that, you need some intensity, like zone 2 and a few harder efforts. Think of walking instead as the foundation the rest is built on.

One last point: if you already walk a lot and still feel exhausted, the problem lies elsewhere — the real causes of fatigue that won't go away are rarely just a lack of steps.

Key takeaways

  • Aim for 7,000 to 8,000 steps a day: that's where most of the health benefits are, and the curve flattens after that.
  • The 10,000-step goal comes from a Japanese marketing slogan, not from science.
  • Even 4,000 steps beat being sedentary — and it's at the bottom of the scale that every step counts most.

Frequently asked questions

How many steps a day do you need to be healthy?

Around 7,000 to 8,000 steps: that's where most of the health benefits are concentrated. Beyond that, the curve flattens (a little later if you're under 60, around 8,000-10,000); below it, every extra thousand steps counts a lot, especially if you're starting from the sedentary zone.

Do you really need to walk 10,000 steps a day?

No. The 10,000 steps come from a 1960s Japanese advertising slogan, not from a scientific recommendation. Most of the benefits are already there at around 7,000-8,000 steps.

Does walking help you lose weight?

Walking raises your background energy expenditure (NEAT), which weighs in your daily total. It helps, but fat loss depends first and foremost on your overall calorie deficit, not on walking alone.
