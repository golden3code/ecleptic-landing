Alcohol and sleep: what really happens during the night

Alcohol can help you fall asleep, but it fragments the second half of the night: less REM sleep, weaker heart recovery. Here's what happens, drink by drink.

By Auguste Phily-Priou, founder of Ecleptic  ·  updated September 30, 2026

Alcohol is one of the most widely used sleep aids — and one of the worst. The paradox is well known: alcohol is a sedative and can help you fall asleep, and yet the night that follows is objectively less restorative. It's not just a morning-after impression: it's one of the most visible effects there is in sleep data.

How does alcohol affect sleep? A bad bargain, hour by hour

The night plays out in two acts, as described in a 2013 review that pulled together the studies on the subject:

  • First half of the night: you fall asleep faster, and deep sleep often even increases. On the surface, everything looks fine.
  • Second half: your body has metabolized the alcohol, and the rebound kicks in — nervous system on alert, light and fragmented sleep, repeated awakenings, thirst. REM sleep, concentrated at the end of the night, takes the biggest hit: it's the stage that handles memory and emotional regulation. The result when you wake up: the hour count looks fine, your head says otherwise.

The most recent meta-analysis (2025, 27 studies) pins down the dose: REM sleep is delayed and shortened starting at a low dose, about two standard drinks, and the effect gets worse with each additional drink. Falling asleep faster, on the other hand, only shows up clearly at a high dose, around five drinks.

What alcohol does to your heart rate and HRV overnight

In physiological data, evening alcohol leaves an unmistakable signature, proportional to the dose: a higher nighttime heart rate, crushed HRV, and a readiness score that plunges by morning. In the lab, a 2021 study of 26 adults confirmed it even at moderate doses. In the real world, a Finnish study compared nights with and without alcohol in 4,098 employees:

How to limit alcohol's effect on sleep without becoming a monk

The dose is everything, and timing matters almost as much:

  • Timing matters: your liver clears alcohol at a fixed rate — blood alcohol only falls by 0.10 to 0.20 g/L per hour (about 0.01% to 0.02% BAC), according to a benchmark review (2010). Allow at least one hour per standard drink, often more. Two drinks finished at 7 p.m. are largely metabolized by bedtime; the same two at 11 p.m. take up a good part of your night.
  • The simple rule: earlier, less, with water and food. One early drink with dinner weighs little, but not nothing; three late drinks, never.
  • Pick your nights: avoid late drinking the night before a big workout or an important day — that's precisely when a good night is worth the most.
  • Don't “pay it back” by sleeping in the next day: you'd add social jet lag to the bill. Get up at your usual time, and take a short nap if you need one.

Key takeaways

  • Alcohol can speed up falling asleep, but it breaks up the second half of the night — especially REM sleep, which takes a hit from two drinks.
  • Higher heart rate, crushed HRV: a night of drinking shows up in the data, dose by dose.
  • The two levers: how much, and above all when. Early and light changes almost everything.

Why does my watch show a bad night after drinking?
