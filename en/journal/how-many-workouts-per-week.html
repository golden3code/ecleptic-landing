How many workouts per week do you need to make progress?

How many workouts per week? From 2 to 5, depending on your level and your goal: what the WHO, the ACSM and the research say about strength training and cardio.

By Auguste Phily-Priou, founder of Ecleptic  ·  updated September 30, 2026

The short answer: 3 to 5 workouts per week are enough to make serious progress — and for most people, 3 well-built, well-recovered sessions beat 6 sessions piled onto a body that can't keep up. Frequency isn't a goal in itself: it's a tool for spreading out the training volume you can absorb.

How many days a week should you lift weights?

The research has settled it: at equal weekly volume, frequency makes little difference. A 2019 meta-analysis (25 studies) found no significant difference in muscle growth by training frequency when volume was equal, and the 2026 position stand from the American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) confirms it: what matters is at least 10 sets per muscle per week for muscle size, and at least 2 sessions per week for strength. Volume comes first; frequency just distributes it. Training each muscle twice a week remains the practical benchmark: it's the simplest way to split that volume into manageable sessions. The ACSM (2009) guidelines by level:

  • Beginner: 2 to 3 full-body sessions per week, with a rest day between each one. It's the simplest formula, and one of the most effective.
  • Intermediate: 3 to 4 sessions, either full body or an upper/lower split. Each muscle trained twice a week.
  • Advanced: 4 to 5 sessions, because you need more volume to keep progressing — not because “more days” is somehow magic.

A muscle you've trained hard needs time to repair. Training every day is possible; training the same thing every day is not.

How often should you do cardio? The 80/20 rule

For endurance, frequency can go higher (low-intensity cardio is absorbed quickly), but how you distribute intensity is decisive: roughly 80% of sessions at an easy intensity — you can talk in full sentences — and 20% at high intensity. That's the distribution Stephen Seiler (2010) describes in national- and international-level endurance athletes. The classic mistake of the motivated amateur: running everything “moderately hard,” a zone that's very tiring without being either truly easy or truly hard.

How many workouts is too many? Your recovery decides

The right frequency isn't a universal number: it's the balance point between your training load and your life. Four factors move that point: your sleep, your calories, your stress and your training history. Two simple checks to tell whether your current frequency is right:

  • You're progressing (weights, paces, reps) from week to week → it's right.
  • You're stalling, your vitals are getting worse, your drive is fading → you're beyond what you can recover from (the specific signs here). Cut back before you add.

Key takeaways

  • 3 to 5 workouts per week cover the vast majority of goals; at equal volume, frequency matters little, and training each muscle twice a week remains the practical benchmark.
  • For cardio: about 80% easy sessions, 20% hard — not everything “moderately hard.”
  • The ideal frequency is the one your recovery can handle while you keep progressing.

Frequently asked questions

How many workouts per week do you need to make progress?

3 to 5 sessions are enough to make serious progress. For most people, 3 well-built, well-recovered sessions beat 6 sessions piled onto a body that can't keep up.

How many times a week should you train each muscle?

Twice a week is the practical benchmark. At equal volume, a 2019 meta-analysis (25 studies) found no clear difference by frequency: what matters first is volume, at least 10 sets per muscle per week according to the American College of Sports Medicine (2026). Two sessions simply make that volume easier to absorb.

Is it a good idea to work out every day?

It's possible, on two conditions: don't train the same thing every day, and keep about 80% of your cardio sessions at an easy intensity — the kind where you can talk in full sentences.
