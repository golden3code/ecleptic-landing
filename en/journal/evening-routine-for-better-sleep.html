The ideal evening routine for better sleep

A 30- to 60-minute wind-down window before bed: low light, a cool bedroom, screens set aside, slow breathing. The bedtime routine that helps you fall asleep.

By Auguste Phily-Priou, founder of Ecleptic

The short answer: give yourself a 30- to 60-minute “wind-down window” before bed — low light, screens set aside, a cool bedroom — so your body can shift from “day” mode to “sleep” mode. You can't fall asleep on command: sleep comes when your nervous system slows down and your internal clock gets the right signals. An evening routine isn't an optional wellness ritual; it's the deceleration ramp that makes falling asleep easy instead of forced.

The principle is simple: every evening, the same steps, in the same time window. Repetition teaches your brain that sleep comes after this sequence — and it starts getting ready on its own.

It doesn't need to be long or elaborate. Three or four simple steps, kept up every night, beat a perfect hour-long routine you drop after a week. The goal is consistency, not perfection.

What light and temperature help you sleep?

These are the two most powerful signals for your clock, and the easiest to control:

  • Dim the lights from early evening. Switch off the ceiling light and move to a low, warm lamp: an international expert consensus (2022) recommends very dim light during the 3 hours before bed. Bright evening light slows melatonin — and what matters most is intensity and room lighting, more than the blue light from screens alone.
  • Bedroom between 17 and 19 °C (63-66 °F). To fall asleep, your body needs to shed a little core temperature; a cool room makes that shift easier.
  • Warm shower or bath 1 to 2 hours before. Counterintuitive but effective: the vasodilation that follows speeds up the drop in temperature and helps you fall asleep faster. A 2019 meta-analysis (17 studies) confirms it.

How to wind down before bed

Once the lights are dimmed, fill that window with what brings you down — and avoid what revs you up:

  • Set screens aside. The problem isn't so much the screen as activating content: social media, news, work email. Replace scrolling with a book, a calm podcast, soft music.
  • Breathe slowly. A few minutes of slow-paced breathing, with the exhale longer than the inhale, shifts your nervous system into recovery mode — that's the whole point of resonance breathing.
  • Empty your head onto paper. Write down your three tasks for tomorrow or whatever is bothering you: it keeps your mind from racing once the lights are off. In a sleep lab, 57 young adults who spent 5 minutes writing a specific to-do list fell asleep faster than those who wrote down what they had already done.
  • Pick a fixed transition activity. Always the same one — a few pages of reading, gentle stretching, a caffeine-free herbal tea. Repeated every night, it becomes a signal your brain eventually recognizes.

What should you avoid before bed?

A good routine is useless if you wreck it with one of these classic mistakes:

  • Late alcohol. It puts you to sleep faster but wrecks the second half of the night — the details are here. The “nightcap to help you sleep” is a trap.
  • The afternoon coffee, still active come evening; and a heavy or very late dinner, which keeps digestion and body temperature up at the wrong time.
  • Intense exercise in the hour before bed, which raises body temperature and alertness. Earlier is no problem: a 2019 meta-analysis (23 studies) concludes that evening exercise doesn't harm sleep, except for an intense workout finished less than an hour before bedtime.
  • Going to bed when you're not sleepy. Lying in bed ruminating teaches your brain that bed is a place for being awake. Go to bed when drowsiness sets in, not just because “it's time”: that's one of the instructions the American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends for insomnia.

Why a regular bedtime matters most

An evening routine works best when it kicks in at about the same time every night. Your internal clock loves predictability: in a cohort of 60,977 participants tracked with accelerometers, sleep regularity predicted mortality risk better than sleep duration did. It's probably the highest-return lever of all — regularity carries more weight than duration. And don't forget your wake-up time: getting up at about the same time every morning, weekends included, anchors your clock even more strongly than your bedtime.

Key takeaways

  • A 30- to 60-minute wind-down window, the same steps every night: low light, bedroom at 17-19 °C, screens set aside.
  • Slow down body and mind: warm shower, reading, slow breathing; avoid alcohol, late coffee and intense exercise in the hour before bed.
  • Start the routine at a regular time: regularity is what makes the biggest difference.

Frequently asked questions

How do you build a good bedtime routine?

Set aside a 30- to 60-minute wind-down window before bed, with the same steps every night: dim the lights, cool the bedroom, set screens aside, and slow down with reading or slow breathing.

What should you do before bed to sleep well?

Dim the lights from early evening, ideally during the 3 hours before bed, aim for a bedroom at 17-19 °C, take a warm shower 1 to 2 hours before, and avoid late alcohol, afternoon coffee and intense exercise in the hour before bedtime.

How long should an evening routine last?

About 30 to 60 minutes is enough: long enough to let your nervous system slow down. The most important thing is to start it at about the same time every night.
