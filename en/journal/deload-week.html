Deload week: when to take one and how to do it

Cut your training volume by 40-50% every 4 to 8 weeks, or as soon as fatigue builds up. Keep some intensity. And no, a deload week isn't being lazy.

By Auguste Phily-Priou, founder of Ecleptic

The short answer: a deload week is a week where you deliberately cut your training volume by about 40 to 50% to let your recovery catch up. Schedule one roughly every 4 to 8 weeks, or as soon as signs of fatigue start piling up. It's neither laziness nor an admission of weakness: it's exactly what lets you keep progressing instead of stalling or getting hurt.

The principle is simple. Training creates fatigue and an adaptation signal. Progress doesn't happen during the session, it happens during recovery. If you stack hard weeks without ever easing off, fatigue ends up masking your gains: you work hard, but you recover less and less. A deload reopens the gap between your training load and your capacity to absorb it — that's the idea behind supercompensation. The European and American consensus statement on overtraining (2013) puts it this way: successful training must include overload, but never the combination of excessive overload and inadequate recovery; short-term fatigue that you fully recover from then turns into progress.

When should you take a deload week?

There are two ways to decide, best used together:

  • By the calendar: every 4 to 8 weeks of serious training. In a 2024 survey of 246 strength and physique athletes, deloads came around every 5 to 6 weeks on average and lasted about a week. Beginners and lighter programs can go longer; the heavier and harder you train, the more often you'll need to deload.
  • By listening to the signals: worsening sleep, flagging motivation, performance slipping session after session, lingering soreness, irritability. These are the signs of accumulated fatigue — when several of them light up, don't negotiate: deload.

The right reflex: don't wait until you're wiped out. A planned deload costs you one quiet week; overreaching that has taken hold costs you several lost weeks, sometimes an injury.

How to do a deload week

The key is to lower the volume while keeping some intensity. You want to rest without losing conditioning — without losing the feel of your lifts or your strength.

  • Cut volume by 40 to 50%. Half as many sets, or fewer exercises per session. That's the main lever. The benchmark comes from tapering before competition: a 2007 meta-analysis (27 studies) found the best results when volume was cut by 41 to 60%, without touching intensity or frequency.
  • Keep some intensity. Keep lifting decent weights, but for very few sets, far from failure. Your body keeps the I know how to do this signal without the exhaustion.
  • Never go to failure. All week, leave reps in reserve. The goal is to recover, not to test your limits.
  • The rest follows: sleep more, eat normally, keep moving. A deload works because your whole body gets a breather at the same time.

One week is enough in the vast majority of cases. Many athletes report feeling better when they get back to training, but the science is still thin: the only controlled trial (2024, 39 trainees) tested a week of complete rest in the middle of 9 weeks of strength training, with no extra muscle gain and slightly less strength at the end. One more argument for cutting back rather than stopping altogether.

Deloading and progressive overload: two sides of the same coin

A deload only makes sense because you push hard the rest of the time. It's the counterweight to progressive overload: you gradually increase the demand over several weeks, then ease off to absorb all that work. Without overload, there's nothing to deload from; without deloads, overload eventually breaks you. Together they create progress in waves — you climb, you catch your breath, you climb higher — far more effective than a straight line that keeps rising until it snaps.

Key takeaways

  • A deload means cutting volume by 40-50%, every 4 to 8 weeks or as soon as fatigue builds up.
  • Lower the volume but keep some intensity, without ever going to failure — you recover without losing conditioning.
  • It isn't laziness: it's the quiet week that makes supercompensation and long-term progress possible.

Frequently asked questions

What is a deload week?

A week where you deliberately cut your training volume by about 40 to 50% to let your recovery catch up with accumulated fatigue. You keep some intensity so you don't lose conditioning.

How often should you deload?

Roughly every 4 to 8 weeks of serious training, or as soon as signs of fatigue build up: worse sleep, dropping motivation, slipping performance. Better to plan ahead than to be forced into it by overreaching.

How do you do a deload week?

Cut volume by 40 to 50% (half as many sets), keep decent weights for a few sets, and never go to failure. Sleep and eat normally to give your whole body a breather.
