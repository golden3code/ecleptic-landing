Smoking, nicotine and exercise: how do they affect performance?

Smoking, nicotine and exercise: carbon monoxide drags down VO2 max, nicotine slows recovery. The real effects on performance, and why quitting pays off fast.

By Auguste Phily-Priou, founder of Ecleptic

The short answer: smoking clearly degrades performance and recovery, on several fronts at once. The carbon monoxide in smoke takes the place of oxygen in your blood, nicotine constricts your blood vessels, and the whole package disrupts your sleep. For an athlete, it's a measurable handicap — and the good news is that it's largely reversible once you quit.

Let's stick to the facts, without moralizing: the idea isn't to make anyone feel guilty, but to understand through which mechanisms tobacco and nicotine weigh on your athletic body, and what that changes in practice.

How does smoking affect endurance?

  • Carbon monoxide steals oxygen's place. It binds to hemoglobin far more readily than oxygen does, so your blood carries less oxygen with every beat. The direct result: less oxygen to your muscles, a lower VO2 max and getting out of breath faster during exercise. A 1999 review confirms that this CO reduces maximal aerobic capacity and brings on fatigue sooner.
  • Your airways get irritated. Smoke attacks the bronchial tubes and eventually reduces lung capacity — endurance takes a hit, and not just in heavy smokers. In U.S. cohorts totaling 25,352 people, the decline in lung function among people smoking fewer than five cigarettes a day reached 68% of the decline seen in those smoking at least thirty.
  • Your heart works harder for less. At the same effort, it has to make up for the oxygen shortfall, which makes every session more costly.

In practice: same legs, same training, but a lower ceiling and sessions that feel harder. And the effect isn't limited to maximal efforts: even an easy run needs oxygen, so even your base endurance suffers. Smoking doesn't just rob you of performance — it robs you of room to improve.

Does nicotine still affect you without the smoke?

This is the part vaping doesn't fix. Nicotine, separated from smoke, keeps its own effects on your athletic body:

  • Vasoconstriction: it constricts the blood vessels of the skin and heart, and speeds up your heart rate. But it dilates the vessels in your muscles, and according to a 2016 review, without combustion, its cardiovascular risks remain low compared with cigarettes. Its real cost to your recovery runs through sleep.
  • Stimulant: it disrupts both falling asleep and sleep quality, especially when used in the evening — a 2009 review (52 studies) found that users take longer to fall asleep, have more fragmented sleep and get less deep sleep. And poor sleep means recovery sabotaged at the root.
  • Dependence: it's strongly habit-forming, which makes quitting hard and keeps you using.

Vaping and nicotine pouches eliminate carbon monoxide and combustion tar — a real advantage on that specific point — but they keep the nicotine, and with it the vasoconstriction, the effects on sleep and the dependence. "Smoke-free" doesn't mean "effect-free."

How smoking and nicotine show up in your data

If you track your vitals, tobacco and nicotine leave readable traces: often lower heart rate variability (a sign of a less well-regulated nervous system, found in the vast majority of studies according to a 2013 review), a resting heart rate that tends to be pulled upward, and more fragmented sleep on the nights you've used. These aren't judgments, they're measurements — and they move when your consumption moves.

A note for occasional smokers: even "social" smoking on the weekend leaves a mark on those nights. You don't need a pack a day for the effect to show up in your sleep and in your recovery the next day.

The good news: how quickly quitting smoking pays off

This is the motivating part, by far. The body repairs a good share of the damage, often much faster than you'd imagine. Here's the WHO timeline:

Source: World Health Organization (2020).
After your last cigaretteWhat changes
20 minutesHeart rate and blood pressure drop
12 hoursCarbon monoxide in the blood returns to normal
2 to 12 weeksCirculation improves, lung function increases
1 to 9 monthsCoughing and shortness of breath decrease
1 yearCoronary heart disease risk drops to about half that of a smoker

So within twelve hours, your blood carries oxygen normally again; within a few weeks, many people describe workouts that "flow" again and getting their breath back. Recovery and sleep then normalize as nicotine leaves the equation, after a few sometimes restless nights during withdrawal.

In other words, every week without tobacco is a nearly free performance gain — one of the rare kinds of "progress" you get without training more. If you want to quit and dependence makes it hard, a doctor or a smoking cessation specialist can support you: it's an area where help makes a real difference.

Key takeaways

  • Smoking hurts endurance: carbon monoxide reduces oxygen transport and lowers VO2 max.
  • Nicotine on its own (vaping included) keeps its effects: constricted vessels, faster heart rate, disrupted sleep — so slower recovery — and dependence.
  • Quitting improves breathing capacity within a few weeks; for dependence, a doctor or a smoking cessation specialist can help.

Frequently asked questions

How does smoking affect athletic performance?

Smoking reduces performance and recovery: carbon monoxide cuts oxygen transport and lowers VO2 max, smoke irritates the airways, and nicotine disrupts sleep, which slows recovery.

Is vaping less bad than smoking for exercise?

Vaping eliminates carbon monoxide and combustion tar, which is an advantage. But it keeps the nicotine, so the vasoconstriction, the effects on sleep and the dependence are all still there.

How long does it take to get your breath back after quitting smoking?

According to the WHO, carbon monoxide in the blood returns to normal within 12 hours, and circulation and lung function improve within 2 to 12 weeks. Coughing and shortness of breath then decrease between 1 and 9 months, and the benefits keep adding up over time.
