Why am I always tired? The real causes of constant fatigue

Hidden sleep debt, irregular hours, undereating, overreaching, deficiencies: the most common causes of fatigue that won't go away, in the order to check them.

By Auguste Phily-Priou, founder of Ecleptic  ·  updated September 30, 2026

“I sleep, but I'm still tired.” It's probably the sentence you hear most as soon as the topic of energy comes up. Constant fatigue rarely has an exotic cause: in the vast majority of cases, it's one of the following five — and often two or three at once. In the order you should check them:

1. You sleep less than you think

This is the first cause to check, because lack of sleep is massive: according to Santé publique France, France's national public health agency (12,637 adults), 18- to 75-year-olds sleep an average of 6 hours 42 minutes a day on weekdays, and 35.9% sleep 6 hours or less. The classic trap: confusing time in bed with time asleep. In 669 Americans wearing an actigraph, 7 hours 30 minutes in bed yielded only 6 hours 6 minutes of actual sleep on average. Repeated over five nights, that gap adds up to a debt of several hours a week, invisible but very real. Start there: measure your real sleep need and compare it with what you actually sleep, not with what you aim for.

2. Your sleep schedule is all over the place

For the same duration, sleep that shifts every night is less restorative: your circadian clock spends its time chasing your schedule. The typical symptom is fatigue despite nights that are “enough” on paper — and the Monday-morning fog. Regularity changes more than you think.

3. You don't eat enough (or your intake is poorly spread out)

Common in athletes: a prolonged calorie deficit — intentional or not — always ends up costing you energy. The International Olympic Committee made it a syndrome in its own right (2023): when intake doesn't cover the energy cost of training, health and performance deteriorate, in women and men alike. The body cuts its spending wherever it can: thermogenesis, motivation, libido, overall vigor. Typical signs: feeling unusually cold, irritability, every workout turning into a grind, fragmented sleep. If you've been combining heavy training with a small plate for weeks, the fatigue isn't a mystery, it's a bill.

4. You're simply doing too much

Fatigue that's still there when you wake up, soreness that lingers, a resting heart rate above your normal, drive that fades: that's the picture of overreaching — training and life stress exceeding your capacity to recover. The specific signs and the protocol to get out of it are here.

5. A medical cause — to check once everything else is in order

If you sleep enough, on a regular schedule, eat until you're satisfied, and two calm weeks haven't changed anything, it's time to see your doctor for a blood test. The common, easy-to-test suspects: iron deficiency (especially in women and endurance athletes: 15% to 35% of female athletes are deficient, according to a 2019 review), vitamin D (in Europe, 13% of the population is clearly deficient, nearly 18% in winter), thyroid, and sleep apnea if you snore and wake up with a dry mouth and a headache. Iron isn't a detail: in a French trial of 198 fatigued women with low ferritin but no anemia, fatigue dropped by 48% on iron, versus 29% on placebo. But don't take iron blindly: the blood test decides. Fatigue that lasts for months deserves a doctor, not a blog post — this one is here to help you rule out the avoidable causes first.

Key takeaways

  • Check in this order: actual sleep duration → regularity → calories → total load → medical checkup.
  • “Unexplained” fatigue is almost always sleep debt or overreaching in disguise.
  • Two weeks of enough, regular sleep is the best free diagnostic test there is.

Frequently asked questions

Why am I always tired even though I sleep?

The most common trap: confusing time in bed with time asleep. In a cohort of 669 adults wearing an actigraph, 7 hours 30 minutes in bed yielded only 6 hours 6 minutes of actual sleep on average. Repeated over five nights, the gap adds up to a debt of several hours a week, invisible but very real.

When should you see a doctor for constant fatigue?

If you sleep enough and on a regular schedule, eat until you're satisfied, and two calm weeks haven't changed anything: see your doctor for a blood test (iron, vitamin D, thyroid) and sleep apnea screening if you snore and wake up with a dry mouth.

Which deficiencies cause fatigue?

The common, easy-to-test suspects: iron (15% to 35% of female athletes are deficient), vitamin D (13% of Europeans clearly deficient, nearly 18% in winter) and thyroid. Fatigue that lasts for months deserves a doctor.
