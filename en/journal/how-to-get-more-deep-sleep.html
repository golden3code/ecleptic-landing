Deep sleep: how to get more of it

A cool bedroom, fixed sleep times, daytime exercise, an early caffeine cutoff: the levers that really boost deep sleep, and the ones that do nothing.

By Auguste Phily-Priou, founder of Ecleptic  ·  updated September 30, 2026

The short answer: you can't “force” deep sleep directly, but you can create the conditions that maximize it: a cool bedroom, regular sleep times, exercise during the day, and no caffeine in the afternoon. Deep sleep makes up about 15 to 20% of the night in adults, concentrated in the first few hours. It does most of the physical work: growth hormone release, tissue repair, clearing waste from the brain, strengthening the immune system.

First thing to know: the amount of deep sleep isn't a number to chase in absolute terms. It varies a lot from person to person, partly for genetic reasons, and it naturally declines with age: in a benchmark polysomnography study (100 healthy sleepers, 2015), it drops from about 21% of the night before age 30 to 15% after 60. And it regulates itself when conditions are right. If you miss out on deep sleep one night, your brain schedules more of it the next night — that's the rebound. Your job isn't to manufacture it. It's to stop sabotaging it.

The #1 lever for deep sleep: temperature

To sink into sleep, your core body temperature has to drop. Anything that gets in the way of that drop cuts into the first hours of the night — exactly when deep sleep happens:

  • A cool bedroom, around 17 to 19 °C (63 to 66 °F) (the usual benchmark): the single highest-return setting for your sleep.
  • A warm shower or bath 1 to 2 hours before bed: the vasodilation that follows speeds up the drop in temperature. Paradoxical but well documented: according to a 2019 meta-analysis (17 studies), water at 40 to 42.5 °C (104 to 108.5 °F) shortens the time it takes to fall asleep, even with just 10 minutes in it.
  • No vigorous exercise in the hour before bed. Evening exercise isn't the enemy people think it is: another 2019 meta-analysis (23 studies) shows it even slightly increases deep sleep; only vigorous exercise that ends less than an hour before bedtime can delay falling asleep.

What reduces deep sleep?

  • Alcohol in the evening — the most deceptive saboteur. It helps you drop off faster and even boosts deep sleep early in the night, but a 2013 review of all the studies in healthy volunteers shows that at every dose, it fragments the second half of the night and delays REM sleep; here's the drink-by-drink breakdown.
  • Erratic sleep times. Deep sleep is scheduled by your circadian clock: when you go to bed at a different time every night, sleep pressure and your body clock fall out of sync, and the structure of the night gets disorganized. Regularity changes more than duration does.
  • Late caffeine. A 2023 meta-analysis (24 studies) concluded that coffee should be drunk at least 8.8 hours before bed so it doesn't shorten your night — and caffeine cuts deep sleep by 11 minutes on average. A coffee at 4 p.m. is too late for a midnight bedtime: have your last coffee 8 to 10 hours before bed.
  • Eating a heavy meal late. Ongoing digestion keeps your temperature and heart rate higher — ideally, have dinner 2 to 3 hours before bed.

Can your smartwatch measure deep sleep accurately?

Be careful. Consumer trackers estimate sleep stages from movement and heart rate — that's an estimate, not a measurement of brain waves. A 2021 study that compared seven consumer devices with polysomnography found them good at detecting sleep, but inconsistent at splitting it into stages. The right way to read them: ignore the absolute value for any single night (“42 minutes of deep sleep” doesn't mean much), and look at your trend over several weeks and what moves it — alcohol, sleep times, exercise, stress. Used that way, the data becomes genuinely useful: that's exactly the logic behind a readiness score.

Key takeaways

  • Deep sleep regulates itself when conditions are right: a cool room, regular hours, exercise during the day.
  • The three worst enemies of your night: alcohol in the evening, erratic sleep times, late caffeine.
  • Read your watch's numbers as a trend over weeks, never as the absolute value of a single night.

Frequently asked questions

How do you increase deep sleep?

You don't force it directly: you create the conditions. A cool bedroom (17 to 19 °C is the usual benchmark), regular bedtimes, exercise during the day, no caffeine in the 8 to 10 hours before bed, and little alcohol in the evening. Deep sleep then regulates itself.

How much deep sleep per night is normal?

About 15 to 20% of the night in adults, concentrated in the first few hours: around 21% before age 30 and 15% after 60 in a benchmark study. The exact amount varies from person to person — it's the trend that matters, not a single night's number.

Do smartwatches measure deep sleep accurately?

They estimate it from movement and heart rate, without measuring brain waves: compared with polysomnography, they detect sleep well but split it into stages inconsistently. Reliable as a trend over several weeks, not as an absolute value.
