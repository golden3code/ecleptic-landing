Zone 2 cardio: what is it, and why is it the best cardio?

Zone 2 is easy cardio where you can still talk in full sentences. Here's why this low intensity builds your aerobic base better than anything else.

By Auguste Phily-Priou, founder of Ecleptic  ·  updated September 30, 2026

The short answer: zone 2 is the low intensity at which you can hold a conversation in full sentences — breathing harder, but able to talk without stopping. As a rough guide, it falls around 60 to 70% of your maximum heart rate, just below the first ventilatory threshold, where fat still supplies a large share of your energy. It sounds too easy to do anything. It's exactly the opposite: it's the foundation of all your fitness.

The trap for the motivated beginner is doing everything “moderately hard.” Zone 2 asks you to ease off — often to the point of finding it frustrating for the first few weeks. But this is the pace where the most rewarding long-term adaptations are built.

Why is zone 2 training so effective?

At low intensity, your body runs on its aerobic system, the one that produces energy using oxygen. And that's where the deepest adaptations happen:

  • It increases your number of mitochondria — the tiny power plants in your cells. The more you have, the longer you can produce energy without wearing out. A 2018 review concludes that training volume is the main thing that drives their number up: and zone 2 is the intensity that lets you accumulate a lot of it.
  • It improves your ability to use fat as fuel, which spares your glycogen stores for the moments that matter. A 2002 study shows that fat oxidation stays high across a wide range of low intensities, peaks around 74% of max heart rate, then drops sharply above that.
  • It builds a denser network of small blood vessels and strengthens your heart, which pumps more blood with every beat.
  • It's quick to recover from. Unlike hard sessions, you can do a lot of it without digging deeper into your recovery debt.

The end result is counterintuitive: a big zone 2 base eventually raises your VO2 max and your performance in hard efforts, because it enlarges the engine everything else runs on.

The talk test: how to tell you're in zone 2

Forget the heart rate monitor for a moment. The most reliable gauge — and it's free — is the talk test: at the right intensity, you can say a full sentence without stopping to catch your breath in the middle, but you couldn't sing. If you have to break up your sentences to breathe, you're too high. If you could recite a poem effortlessly, you're too low. A 2014 review backs this up: as long as you can talk comfortably, you stay below the ventilatory threshold, and the test holds up whether you're walking, running or cycling.

Another useful sign: you should finish your session thinking, “I could have kept going.” Zone 2 should never leave you on your knees. On a watch, a steady heart rate that doesn't drift upward as the minutes go by confirms you're holding the right pace.

What is the 80/20 rule in endurance training?

National- and international-level endurance athletes do roughly 80% of their sessions at low intensity and only 20% at high intensity: that's the distribution physiologist Stephen Seiler described in his 2010 review, and it also works as a compass for recreational athletes. It's no accident: this mix maximizes adaptations while keeping fatigue in check. Zone 2 makes up the bulk of that 80%.

Most people make the opposite mistake: too much moderate intensity, not enough truly easy or truly hard work. The result is a lot of fatigue for little progress. Slowing down your easy sessions is often what gets you improving again.

How much zone 2 per week, and how to start

  • 2 to 3 sessions of 30 to 45 minutes a week are enough to build a base; the World Health Organization (WHO) sets the health benchmark at 150-300 minutes of moderate activity a week, walking included. Brisk walking, cycling, rowing, slow running: the mode matters little, it's the intensity that does the work.
  • Stay strictly in the zone, even if your ego complains. On hills, walk if you need to so you don't drift above it.
  • Be patient. The benefits build over weeks and months, not three sessions. The good news: they add up, and they last.

You don't need fancy equipment to get started. If you want a number, estimate your maximum heart rate with 208 − 0.7 × your age, the formula from a 2001 meta-analysis (351 studies, 18,712 subjects). It's more accurate than the classic 220 minus your age, which underestimates it in older people. Aim for 60 to 70% of that number — but keep the talk test as the final judge, because any formula is only an approximation. The hardest part is mental: accepting going slowly when every instinct tells you to speed up. Many runners find they have to alternate slow running and walking to stay low enough for the first few weeks. That's normal, and it improves quickly: as your base grows, you'll go faster at the same heart rate.

Zone 2 fits easily into a week without blowing up your training load — useful when you're looking for the right number of sessions to sustain over time.

Key takeaways

  • Zone 2 = low intensity (~60-70% of max heart rate) where you can talk in full sentences without getting out of breath.
  • It builds mitochondria, your aerobic base and your ability to burn fat — the foundation that also improves your hard efforts.
  • Aim for 2-3 sessions of 30-45 minutes a week, within an 80% easy / 20% hard split.

Frequently asked questions

What is zone 2 cardio?

The low intensity at which you can hold a conversation in full sentences without getting out of breath, around 60 to 70% of your maximum heart rate. It's the foundation of endurance: it builds mitochondria and your aerobic base.

How do I know if I'm in zone 2?

Use the talk test: you can say a full sentence without stopping to catch your breath in the middle, but you couldn't sing. If you have to break up your sentences to breathe, you're too high; if you can sing, you're too low.

How much zone 2 should you do per week?
