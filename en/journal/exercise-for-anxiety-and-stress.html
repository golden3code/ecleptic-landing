Exercise for anxiety: what does the science say?

Regular exercise reduces anxiety and mild depression, sometimes as much as other standard approaches. The mechanisms, the right dose and the limits.

By Auguste Phily-Priou, founder of Ecleptic

The short answer: yes, and the evidence is solid. Regular exercise reduces anxiety and depressive symptoms — it's one of the best-established findings in mental health research. An umbrella review of 97 meta-analyses (1,039 trials, 128,119 participants), published in 2023 in the British Journal of Sports Medicine, found a medium, clear effect on anxiety as well as on depression. For depression, a 2024 meta-analysis in The BMJ (218 trials) concludes that walking or jogging, yoga and strength training deserve a place alongside first-line treatments, psychotherapy and antidepressants. That's considerable for an intervention that's free, has minimal side effects and benefits the rest of your body too.

One nuance needs to come right away, because it matters: “alongside” doesn't mean “instead of.” Exercise is a powerful lever, not a treatment that replaces professional care. With a clinical anxiety disorder or established depression, the right approach combines physical activity with support from a professional — exercise on top, not instead.

How does exercise reduce anxiety? Four mechanisms

  • Your brain reorganizes. Exercise stimulates neurotrophic factors (like BDNF) that support plasticity and neuron health, especially in the areas involved in mood regulation.
  • Your stress system recalibrates. Regular activity teaches your body to switch on and then switch off its stress response more efficiently — the cortisol axis becomes less reactive day to day.
  • Your sleep improves. Poor sleep feeds anxiety, which in turn degrades sleep: exercise breaks that loop at one of the few points you truly control.
  • Your sense of control comes back. Setting a workout and sticking to it, watching your performance improve, taking back charge of your body: that “I can do something” effect counts as much as the biochemistry.

How much exercise helps anxiety? Less than you might think

Good news: you don't need to wear yourself out to get the benefits. Here's what the data show:

  • No need for maximum intensity. In a Swedish trial run in primary care (2022, 286 patients with anxiety), 12 weeks of exercise clearly reduced anxiety, whether training was light or vigorous — higher intensity giving a slight bonus. Consistency, on the other hand, isn't negotiable.
  • Cardio and strength training both work. Endurance exercise has the most evidence for anxiety, but a 2017 meta-analysis (16 studies, 922 participants) shows that strength training reduces it too. The better of the two is the one you'll do with enjoyment and consistency.
  • A reasonable benchmark: the World Health Organization's, 150 to 300 minutes of moderate activity per week, spread over several sessions. But the first walk already counts: according to a meta-analysis of 36 randomized trials (2015), a single session eases in-the-moment anxiety a little — a modest effect that consistency amplifies.

Watch out for the opposite trap: doing too much backfires on your mood. Overtraining degrades sleep, drains you and makes you irritable — anxiety can get worse. Here as elsewhere, it's the balance between training load and recovery that decides.

Is it just endorphins? Forget the myth

People often boil down exercise's effect on mood to “it releases endorphins.” That's too simple. The passing euphoria after a workout is real, but most of the benefit for anxiety isn't that little post-workout hit: it's the deep adaptation, built over weeks of regular practice, that recalibrates your stress system and improves your sleep. In other words, you're not chasing a high — you're building firmer ground. That's why someone who has been moving three times a week for two months is doing better, on average, than someone who did one big workout yesterday and nothing since.

Combine it with breathing

Exercise works on the baseline, over weeks. For acute spikes — stage fright, a knot in your stomach, stress rising before an appointment — there's an immediate, free tool: slow resonance breathing. The two complement each other perfectly: movement for the long-term trend, breath for the emergency.

When should you get help for anxiety?

This article is about well-being and prevention, not medicine. If anxiety keeps you from functioning day to day, has lasted for weeks, or comes with panic attacks, dark thoughts or deep distress, exercise isn't enough of an answer: talk to a doctor or a psychologist. If you are having suicidal thoughts or are in crisis, call a crisis line right away — 988 in the US, 116 123 (Samaritans) in the UK and Ireland, free and available 24/7 — or your local emergency number in an emergency (911 in the US, 999 or 112 in the UK and Europe). Asking for help isn't a sign of weakness — it's the same logic as seeing a physical therapist for an injury that won't heal.

Key takeaways

  • Regular exercise reduces anxiety and depressive symptoms; for depression, it ranks alongside first-line treatments — without replacing them.
  • It works through the brain (BDNF), stress regulation, sleep and your sense of control; consistency first, intensity as a bonus.
  • If anxiety keeps you from living normally, exercise isn't enough: see a professional, and call a crisis line (988 in the US, 116 123 in the UK and Ireland) if you have suicidal thoughts.

Frequently asked questions

Does exercise help with anxiety?

Yes: regular exercise reduces anxiety and depressive symptoms, with a medium, clear effect according to an umbrella review of 97 meta-analyses. It works through the brain, stress, sleep and your sense of control — without replacing professional care if you have a disorder.

What is the best exercise for stress and anxiety?

Cardio and strength training both work; endurance exercise has the most evidence for anxiety. No need to wear yourself out: light but regular training already reduces anxiety, and more vigorous intensity adds a small bonus.

How much exercise per week for anxiety?

The WHO's health benchmark is 150 to 300 minutes of moderate activity per week, spread over several sessions. But a single session already brings modest relief: the first walk counts.
